\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The geometry a quantum kernel imposes on its data is decided by the encoding, before
training begins. For hierarchical data that geometry should be ultrametric, and we have
shown that the encodings in common use cannot make it so. Product feature maps fail for
a structural reason --- multiplicativity across digits is incompatible with resolving a
tree beyond a bounded depth --- and encodings of insufficient width fail for a reason
that does not depend on their structure at all, which is what rules out the entangling
maps the first argument cannot reach.

The positive result is that path states close the gap exactly. They realise precisely
the strictly monotone ultrametric kernels, on arbitrary rooted trees, using a number of
qubits within an additive constant of the proven lower bound, with $n+1$ non-zero
amplitudes and $O(n \log \abs{V})$ gates. Their ultrametricity is immune to global
depolarising noise, whose effect on distances is affine, and survives finite sampling
at a measured budget of $\ShotBudget$ shots per kernel entry.

What this does not deliver is a faster kernel. The construction's value is as a
constraint on encoding design and as an input stage for models where no closed form
survives, and as an argument that the encoding deserves to be
chosen for the geometry of the data rather than for the convenience of the circuit.

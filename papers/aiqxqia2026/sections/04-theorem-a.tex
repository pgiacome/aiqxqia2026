\section{A No-Go Theorem for Product Encodings}
\label{sec:theorem-a}

\begin{theorem}[Product no-go]
  \label{thm:product-nogo}
  Let $\Phi$ be a block-product feature map on $\Zpn$ (\Cref{def:block-product-map})
  whose fidelity kernel is ultrametric with profile $f$. Put
  $v^{*} = \min\{\, v : f(v) > 0 \,\}$ and assume $v^{*} < n$, and let $B^{*}$ be the
  block containing level $v^{*}+1$. Then for every block $B$ all of whose levels exceed
  $\max B^{*}$, and all digit tuples $a, b$,
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:trivial-block}
    \abs{\braket{\psi_B(a)}{\psi_B(b)}} = 1 .
  \end{equation}
  Consequently $f(v) = 1$ for every $v \ge \max B^{*}$: the map resolves the tree only
  to depth $\max B^{*}$, and its resolution depth is at most $\abs{B^{*}} + 2$.

  For a \emph{product} map every block is a single level, so $\max B^{*} = v^{*}+1$ and
  $f$ takes at most the three values $\{0, f(v^{*}), 1\}$. In particular no product map
  realises a strictly monotone profile once $n \ge 2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The argument uses exactly one property of the encoding: fidelity is multiplicative
  across tensor factors, \cref{eq:multiplicative}.

  Choose leaves $x, y$ that agree on levels $1, \dots, v^{*}$, differ at level
  $v^{*}+1$, and agree at every level above that; such a pair exists because
  $v^{*} < n$. Then $\lcadepth(x,y) = v^{*}$, and every factor except $B^{*}$
  contributes $g(x,x) = 1$, so
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:base-value}
    f(v^{*}) = K(x, y)
    = \fid{\psi_{B^{*}}(x_{B^{*}})}{\psi_{B^{*}}(y_{B^{*}})} > 0 .
  \end{equation}

  Now let $B$ be a block whose levels all exceed $\max B^{*}$, fix digit tuples
  $a \ne b$ for it, and let $y'$ agree with $y$ everywhere except on $B$, where
  $y'_B = b$ while $x_B = a$. Because $x$ and $y'$ still first differ at level
  $v^{*}+1$, and $B$ lies strictly above $B^{*}$, we still have
  $\lcadepth(x, y') = v^{*}$ and therefore $K(x, y') = f(v^{*})$. Multiplicativity
  gives
  \begin{equation*}
    K(x, y')
    = \fid{\psi_{B^{*}}(x_{B^{*}})}{\psi_{B^{*}}(y_{B^{*}})} \cdot
      \fid{\psi_{B}(a)}{\psi_{B}(b)}
    = f(v^{*}) \cdot \fid{\psi_B(a)}{\psi_B(b)} .
  \end{equation*}
  Since $f(v^{*}) > 0$ by \cref{eq:base-value}, this forces
  $\fid{\psi_B(a)}{\psi_B(b)} = 1$, which is \cref{eq:trivial-block}. Equality in
  Cauchy--Schwarz for unit vectors then gives
  $\ket{\psi_B(b)} = e^{i\theta}\ket{\psi_B(a)}$: block $B$ carries no information
  about the data.

  Every block beyond $B^{*}$ being trivial, $K$ depends only on the digits in blocks up
  to and including $B^{*}$, that is, on levels $1, \dots, \max B^{*}$. Two leaves
  agreeing down to depth $\max B^{*}$ therefore agree in every non-trivial factor and
  have $K(x,y) = 1$, so $f(v) = 1$ for all $v \ge \max B^{*}$. Together with $f(v) = 0$
  for $v < v^{*}$, only the levels $v^{*} \le v < \max B^{*}$ remain unconstrained, and
  they number at most $\abs{B^{*}}$; the resolution depth is therefore at most
  $\abs{B^{*}} + 2$.

  For a product map $\abs{B^{*}} = 1$ and $\max B^{*} = v^{*}+1$, so the profile is $0$
  below $v^{*}$, equal to $f(v^{*})$ at $v^{*}$, and $1$ from $v^{*}+1$ on: three
  values. A strictly monotone profile has $f(0) > 0$, hence $v^{*} = 0$, and needs
  $n+1$ distinct values, which exceeds three whenever $n \ge 2$.
\end{proof}

The proof turns on multiplicativity alone. It is therefore indifferent to the local
dimensions $d_i$, to whether the local states are real or complex, and to how they were
chosen, which is what makes it a statement about a class of encodings rather than about
particular instances.

\begin{remark}[The block bound is tight, so the three-value claim is genuinely
  about product maps]
  \label{rem:block-tightness}
  Nothing in \Cref{def:block-product-map} forbids the single-block partition
  $\mathcal{B} = \{\{1, \dots, n\}\}$, under which the path state of
  \Cref{thm:path-states} is itself a block-product map realising $n+1$ distinct values.
  The theorem is consistent with this, since $\max B^{*} = n$ makes its conclusion
  vacuous. What it constrains is how far a map can resolve \emph{beyond} the block
  containing the first informative level, which is severe when blocks are small; only at
  $\abs{B^{*}} = 1$ does it collapse to three values.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}
  \label{cor:standard-encodings}
  On $\Zpn$ with $n \ge 2$, none of the following induces a strictly monotone
  ultrametric kernel: angle encoding with one qubit per digit; computational-basis
  encoding; and any first-order Pauli-$Z$ feature map, whose phase is a sum of
  single-digit terms and which is therefore a product map.
\end{corollary}

Basis encoding deserves a sentence, because it is the case that can be mistaken for a
success. It attains $v^{*} = n$, outside the hypothesis of \Cref{thm:product-nogo}: its
kernel is the identity, its induced distance is the discrete metric, and it satisfies
\cref{eq:strong-triangle} with zero violations while resolving nothing. That is why
\Cref{subsec:diagnostics} insists on reporting resolution depth alongside violations.

\begin{remark}[What this theorem does not cover]
  \label{rem:not-covered}
  A ZZ or IQP feature map applies all-pairs couplings between qubits, so its state does
  not factorise across digits and \cref{eq:multiplicative} fails.
  \Cref{thm:product-nogo} says nothing about it. We do not patch the gap by weakening
  the claim; we close it in \Cref{sec:theorem-b} with an argument that ignores the
  structure of the encoding entirely.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Numerical verification.}
In all trials --- five seeds at each of
$(p, n) \in \{(2,3), (3,2), (2,4), (5,2)\}$ --- randomly drawn product maps failed level
constancy
(\testname{test_theorem_a_random_product_maps_are_never_strictly_monotone}). A
product map built to satisfy the conclusion, with one non-trivial level and the rest
trivial, yields exactly two distinct kernel values as predicted
(\testname{test_theorem_a_ultrametric_product_map_resolves_only_one_level}), and
switching on a second non-trivial level destroys level constancy, which is the
contradiction the proof derives
(\testname{test_theorem_a_a_nontrivial_deep_factor_destroys_ultrametricity}).

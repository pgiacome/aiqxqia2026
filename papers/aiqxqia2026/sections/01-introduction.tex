\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A quantum kernel method embeds classical data through a feature map $\Phi$ and compares
two points by the fidelity of their images,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fidelity-kernel}
  K(x, y) = \fid{\Phi(x)}{\Phi(y)} .
\end{equation}
Everything a downstream model can express about the similarity of $x$ and $y$ passes
through this one number, and the number is fixed by the encoding alone: whatever
geometry $\Phi$ imposes is imposed before any parameter is trained
\citep{Schuld2021KernelMethods,HavlicekEtAl2019QuantumEnhanced}. The choice of encoding
is therefore not a preprocessing detail. It is the model's inductive bias.

Much of the data to which quantum machine learning is applied is hierarchical.
Taxonomies, ontologies, phylogenies and type lattices are trees, and the natural
distance on a tree is not Euclidean but \emph{ultrametric}: distances obey the strong
triangle inequality
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:strong-triangle}
  d(x, z) \le \max\bigl(d(x, y),\, d(y, z)\bigr),
\end{equation}
which is strictly stronger than the ordinary triangle inequality. Equivalently, the
points sit at the leaves of a tree and distance decreases with the depth of the lowest
common ancestor. The canonical ultrametric space is the ring of $p$-adic integers
$\Zp$, whose valuation $\vp(x - y)$ counts matching least-significant base-$p$ digits
and so measures exactly that depth. This paper asks which quantum feature maps induce an
exactly ultrametric kernel, and answers with two obstructions and a matching
construction.

\paragraph{Theorem A: product encodings cannot.}
A map that factorises across digits induces a multiplicative kernel, and
multiplicativity bounds how deep it can resolve the tree, whatever the local dimension:
a product map is left with three kernel values, too few for a strictly monotone profile
once $n \ge 2$. The argument extends to disjoint blocks of digits, where the bound is
set by one block's size, so it constrains a class of encodings rather than a list.
Angle encoding is a corollary; computational-basis encoding is excluded by a separate
one-line argument, and is the instructive case that is ultrametric while resolving
nothing.

\paragraph{Theorem B: narrow encodings cannot either, whatever their structure.}
The Gram matrix of any exact realisation is positive semi-definite with unit diagonal,
which forces its rank to at least $L^{2} / \sum_{x,y} K(x,y)$ on $L$ points. On the regular
$p$-ary tree with a geometric profile of parameter $s > 1$ this exceeds $2^{n}$ for
every $p \ge 3$ once $n$ passes an explicit threshold: beyond it, no $n$-qubit
pure-state encoding realises that kernel on $\Zpn$, entangling or not. This is what rules out the IQP and ZZ
feature maps Theorem~A cannot reach.

\paragraph{Theorem C: path states can, at essentially the optimal width on $\Zpn$.}
Writing $\anc_i(x)$ for the depth-$i$ ancestor of a leaf $x$, the state
$\Phi_f(x) = \sum_i a_i \ket{\anc_i(x)}$ realises \emph{every} strictly monotone
ultrametric kernel exactly, with amplitudes read off the target profile. It needs
$\lceil \log_2 \abs{V} \rceil$ qubits on a tree of $\abs{V}$ nodes, $n+1$ non-zero
amplitudes and $O(n \log \abs{V})$ gates. On $\Zpn$ with $s > 1$ that matches
Theorem~B to within an additive constant; on a general tree the two quantities are not
directly comparable.

We also record a fourth fact with a two-line proof: global depolarising noise acts on
the induced distance as a non-decreasing affine map, so it cannot break the strong
triangle inequality or level constancy at any error rate (\Cref{prop:depolarising}).
What it destroys is contrast rather than geometry. Finite sampling behaves in the
opposite way and breaks the inequality immediately, for a reason specific to ultrametric
spaces that \Cref{sec:experiments} makes precise.

\paragraph{What this paper does not claim.}
The kernel of \Cref{thm:path-states} is $K(x,y) = f(\lcadepth(x,y))$, and a lowest
common ancestor is computable classically in time linear in the depth of the tree.
\textbf{There is no quantum speedup in kernel evaluation here, and we claim none.} We
say so in the introduction rather than leaving a reader to discover it, because it
determines how the rest should be read. The contribution is a no-go that constrains
current encoding practice; a dimension bound that applies to arbitrary encodings and so
survives future designs; and an explicit encoding, of optimal width on $\Zpn$, usable
as the input stage of deeper quantum models, where no closed form survives the
subsequent layers.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\textbf{C1}, a no-go for product feature maps (\Cref{thm:product-nogo}) bounding the
resolution depth of any block-product map by one block's size and collapsing to three
kernel values for genuine product maps. \textbf{C2}, a dimension lower bound for
arbitrary feature maps (\Cref{thm:dimension-bound}), ruling out every $n$-qubit encoding
of the geometric profile on $\Zpn$ for $p \ge 3$ past an explicit threshold.
\textbf{C3}, an exact construction (\Cref{thm:path-states}) realising precisely the
strictly monotone ultrametric kernels, matching C2 on $\Zpn$ to within an additive
constant, with a converse characterising every realisation with non-negative overlaps
up to isometry, read modulo the populated levels. \textbf{C4}, its generalisation to arbitrary rooted trees, with $\Zpn$ as the
specialisation in which the profile ties to $\abs{\cdot}_p$. \textbf{C5}, an empirical
study on WordNet, Gene Ontology and the NCBI taxonomy separating ultrametricity from
usable resolution, mapping the expressivity/hierarchy trade-off of a re-uploading
extension, and quantifying the shot budget required.
